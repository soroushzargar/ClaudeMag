Reward-Estimated Hypergradient for Bilevel Reinforcement Learning with Black-Box Follower

Inverse RL with Bias Cancellation and Eigenvalue Truncation Unlocks Bilevel RL Without Access to the Follower’s Reward
NeurIPS 2026 Submission
arXiv:2611.11998 • cs.LG / cs.AI • NeurIPS 2026
Bilevel RL requires the leader to differentiate through the follower’s best-response, which presupposes access to the follower’s reward. RE-HG uses inverse RL to estimate this reward, then cancels reward-shaping bias via dynamic diversity and suppresses Hessian explosions via eigenvalue truncation, achieving hypergradient estimates aligned at 0.91 cosine similarity with a white-box oracle.
At a Glance
Problem: Bilevel RL hypergradient estimation requires the follower’s reward, but in real settings the follower’s reward is hidden.
Why it matters: Mechanism design, incentive alignment, and multi-agent learning all involve a leader optimizing against an unknown follower reward.
Method: Estimate the follower’s reward via IRL; cancel reward-shaping bias by aggregating across diverse dynamics; truncate Hessian eigenvalues to prevent norm explosions.
Result: Hypergradient cosine similarity 0.91 vs. oracle; outperforms oracle on sharp reward landscapes (oracle gets stuck in local minima).
Evidence: Navigation, manipulation, and mechanism design benchmarks with white-box oracle comparison.
The Big Picture

Bilevel reinforcement learning models a hierarchical game: a leader sets parameters θL (a mechanism, an incentive structure, or a training curriculum), and a follower best-responds with policy πF*(θL) that maximizes their own reward RF. The leader’s objective depends on the follower’s response, so optimizing θL requires the hypergradient—the gradient of VL with respect to θL accounting for how changes in θL change πF*.

Computing the hypergradient involves differentiating through the follower’s best-response optimality condition, which requires the follower’s reward RF (to compute the value-function Hessian). In the black-box setting, only follower trajectories are observable, not RF. Real-world applications are predominantly black-box: a government setting tax policy against firms whose profit functions are private; an online platform designing recommendation rewards against users whose true preferences are latent. RE-HG is the first bilevel RL method to handle the black-box setting with rigorous bias correction.

Why Existing Approaches Fall Short

White-box bilevel RL requires RF explicitly. In the absence of RF, the follower’s value-function Hessian cannot be computed.

Naive IRL + hypergradient: Using an IRL-estimated R̂F in place of RF introduces reward-shaping bias. Any function of the form R̂F + γΦ(s') − Φ(s) fits the follower’s trajectories equally well for any potential Φ, yet the resulting hypergradients differ. The gradient estimate is biased by an amount proportional to ∇θLΦ, which can be large.

Model-free bilevel RL avoids IRL but requires differentiating through the follower’s policy directly, which is only feasible when the follower’s policy is a differentiable network under the leader’s control.

Core Mechanism

RE-HG’s three-component correction addresses the three failure modes of naive bilevel RL with an estimated reward:

(1) Inverse RL reward estimation. MaxEnt IRL with function approximation recovers R̂F ≈ RF from follower trajectories. The IRL estimate is updated online and tracks changes in πF*(θL).

(2) Bias cancellation via dynamic diversity. RE-HG collects follower trajectories under K diverse environmental dynamics and averages the resulting IRL rewards:

R̅F = (1/K) ∑k=1K R̂Fk

Under mild conditions, the shaping term satisfies ∑k ∇θLΦk ≈ 0 when the dynamics are sufficiently diverse.

(3) Eigenvalue truncation. The hypergradient involves a matrix inverse (HF)−1. RE-HG replaces it with (HF + εI)−1 where ε is chosen to bound the inverse norm.

Technical Formulation

The leader’s hypergradient via the implicit function theorem:

∇θL VL = ∂θL VL − (dπF*/dθL)T ∇πF VL

RE-HG substitutes R̅F for RF and replaces the Hessian inverse with the truncated version:

ĤF−1 = (HF(R̂F) + εI)−1

The resulting RE-HG hypergradient estimate is then applied in gradient ascent on VL.

Learning or Inference Procedure

RE-HG alternates between: (1) Follower simulation: roll out follower trajectories under K = 5 diverse dynamics for the current θL; update R̂F; (2) Hypergradient estimation: compute RE-HG using the averaged estimated reward and truncated Hessian; (3) Leader update: apply gradient ascent on VL using the RE-HG estimate. No additional models are required beyond the follower policy, the leader policy, and a reward function approximator for IRL.

What the Guarantee Says

Theorem (informal): Under the assumption that follower trajectories from K sufficiently diverse dynamics are collected, the RE-HG gradient estimate converges to the true hypergradient as K → ∞, with bias decreasing as O(1/K) times the shaping gradient’s magnitude. No formal sample complexity bound is provided.

Experimental Findings
  • Gradient alignment: Mean cosine similarity to white-box oracle is 0.91 across tasks; naive IRL baseline achieves 0.63.
  • Smooth landscapes: Final VL is statistically indistinguishable from oracle on 4/5 evaluated smooth-landscape tasks.
  • Sharp landscapes: RE-HG outperforms oracle by 8% on average due to implicit Hessian regularization that prevents the oracle from getting stuck in saddle points.
  • Dynamics diversity K: K = 3 recovers 85% of the bias cancellation; K = 5 achieves 97%; K = 10 adds negligible improvement.
Ablations and Interpretation

Without bias cancellation: Gradient cosine similarity drops from 0.91 to 0.74; on 2/5 tasks the leader converges to the wrong equilibrium.

Without eigenvalue truncation: Training is unstable in 3/5 tasks (Hessian norm exceeds 104); with truncation (ε = 10−3), all 5 tasks converge stably.

IRL architecture choice: MaxEnt IRL with a 2-layer MLP reward function suffices; a 4-layer network improves alignment by <1%; a linear reward function loses 15% alignment.

Dynamic diversity design: Initial-state randomization contributes more to bias cancellation than transition perturbation; combining both achieves better cancellation than either alone.
