{\LARGE\bfseries\raggedright
  Compile by Training: Turning Natural-Language
  Specifications into Local Neural Functions}\par\vspace{4pt}

{\large\itshape\raggedright
  Teacher LLMs Generate Task-Specific Training Data
  at Compile Time; a Lightweight Adapter Runs Offline
  at 83.6\% Semantic Accuracy Where Fast Compilers
  Achieve Zero}\par\vspace{6pt}

{\small\textbf{By} Yuntian Deng, Pengyu Nie, Stuart Shieber
  (Harvard University; et al.)}\par\vspace{2pt}

{\small\color{keywordgray}arXiv:2609.04199 \textbullet{} EMNLP 2026 System Demonstrations}
\par\vspace{2pt}\noindent{\color{rulered}\rule{\columnwidth}{0.6pt}}\vspace{6pt}

A natural-language specification --- ``given an address string, return the
city component, normalised to title case, with abbreviations expanded'' ---
describes a deterministic function. Current practice runs a large frontier
LLM at inference time for every query, incurring repeated cost, requiring
internet connectivity, and providing no persistent, versioned artifact.
Compile by Training (CbT) converts the specification into a lightweight
adapter that runs locally on a compact interpreter model, with no runtime
teacher access. At compile time, teacher LLMs generate diverse
input-output examples that train the adapter to implement the
specification exactly. The result runs at 12\,ms per query with 83.6\%
semantic accuracy on FuzzyBench-Hard --- a benchmark where the
Program-as-Weights fast compiler achieves zero exact matches.

\section{Motivation: From Repeated Inference to Compiled Functions}

For a task that will be called millions of times --- normalising product
names, extracting structured fields from documents, controlling a 3D
avatar from natural language --- the cost of a frontier LLM call per
invocation is prohibitive. The output distribution of the task is fixed
by its specification; there is no need to re-solve it at runtime.

The Program-as-Weights (PAW) framework already embeds this intuition:
a specification is treated as a ``program'' compiled into a neural
network's weights. PAW's fast compiler generates examples in seconds
and fine-tunes a small adapter. But fast compilation is shallow ---
too few examples, too little training --- and fails on hard specifications.

CbT extends PAW with a deeper, more expensive compilation that produces
far higher accuracy while preserving the local, fast, offline runtime.

\section{Compile by Training: The Four Steps}

\textbf{Step 1: Example generation by teacher committee.}
Given specification $s$, a committee of $K$ teacher LLMs is queried
to generate diverse $(x_i, y_i^*)$ input-output pairs. Multiple teachers
reduce individual model errors.

\textbf{Step 2: Validation by cross-reference.}
An input $x$ is accepted with output $y^*$ if at least $k$ of $K$
teachers agree on $y^*$. Disagreed pairs are discarded. The disagreement
rate $d$ serves as a specification ambiguity signal.

\textbf{Step 3: Adapter training on curated examples.}
A LoRA adapter $\delta\theta$ for a compact local interpreter is trained
on the validated dataset $\mathcal{D}_s = \{(x_i, y_i^*)\}$:
\[
  \mathcal{L}_{\text{CbT}} =
  -\sum_{(x,y^*) \in \mathcal{D}_s} \log p_{\theta + \delta\theta}(y^* | s, x)
\]

\textbf{Step 4: Local deployment.}
The compiled artifact $(s, \delta\theta)$ is stored. At inference time,
the compact model with its adapter processes new inputs locally, without
any teacher or internet access, at 12\,ms per query.

\section{Semantic Accuracy Metric}

Standard exact-match fails for tasks with multiple valid output forms.
CbT uses \textbf{semantic accuracy}:
\[
  \text{SemAcc} = \frac{1}{N}
  \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbf{1}[\text{sem-equiv}(f_\theta(x_i), y_i^*)]
\]
where sem-equiv judges equivalence using a judge LLM, accepting
different surface forms of the correct answer.

\section{Accuracy vs.\ Compile Compute}

Empirically, accuracy saturates as a function of training set size:
\[
  \text{SemAcc}(N_{\text{ex}}) \approx A - B \cdot e^{-\alpha N_{\text{ex}}}
\]
with $A \approx 0.85$, $B \approx 0.84$, $\alpha \approx 0.003$ on
FuzzyBench-Hard. Saturation occurs around $N_{\text{ex}} = 1000$
examples, corresponding to a compile time of approximately one minute
(GPT-4o teacher, parallel calls).

\section{Composition}

CbT functions compose by chaining. If $f_A$ implements specification
$s_A$ and $f_B$ implements $s_B$, a composed function $f_{A \circ B}$
is compiled by: (1) generating a composed specification $s_{A \circ B}$;
(2) using $f_A$ to produce intermediate inputs for $f_B$ as training
examples. The resulting adapter implements $s_{A \circ B}$ locally
without the intermediate function at inference time.

\section{Experimental Results}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
Method & SemAcc & Compile & Runtime \\
\midrule
Teacher (GPT-4o) runtime & 91.2\% & 0\,s & 2.1\,s/q \\
PAW fast compiler        & 51.3\% & 8\,s & 12\,ms/q \\
\textbf{CbT (ours)}      & \textbf{83.6\%} & \textbf{60\,s} & \textbf{12\,ms/q} \\
CbT + validation         & 87.1\% & 120\,s & 12\,ms/q \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

CbT closes 80\% of the quality gap between PAW and teacher-at-runtime
while matching PAW's fast local inference. On WebTask, two-function
compositions achieve 76.4\% task success, confirming composition viability.

\section{Ablations}

\textbf{Committee size:} 3 teachers reduces disagreement from 31\%
(1 teacher) to 12\% and improves SemAcc from 79.4\% to 83.6\%.

\textbf{Training set size:} 1000 examples saturates; 500 achieve 80.1\%;
2000 add only 0.8 points.

\textbf{LoRA rank:} Rank 16 achieves full accuracy; rank 4 loses 2.1
points; rank 64 adds nothing.

\textbf{Specification clarity:} Disagreement rate $d > 0.4$ predicts
low SemAcc; prompting users to clarify ambiguous specifications recovers
4.3 SemAcc points on average.

\textbf{Practical applications:} Website form-filling helpers and
language-controlled 3D avatar motion demos confirm that CbT applies
to realistic end-to-end tasks beyond benchmarks.
